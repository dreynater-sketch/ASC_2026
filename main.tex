\title{RF Characterization of a Sputtered Nb-Coated Copper Cavity at 11\,GHz: Benchmarking Against Bulk Copper and BCS Niobium}

\begin{abstract}
Superconducting radio-frequency (RF) cavities can raise the quality factor of a copper haloscope or accelerator cavity by orders of magnitude, but the coating's performance must be separated from the losses of the copper substrate, the cavity seam, and the deposition system before it can be attributed to the film itself. We report the fabrication and 11\,GHz RF characterization of a two-piece hexagonal oxygen-free high-conductivity (OFHC) copper cavity sputter-coated with a \SI{500}{\nano\meter} Ta diffusion barrier and a \SI{700}{\nano\meter} Nb film. The cavity was measured from 2 to \SI{10}{\kelvin} in a Quantum Design Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS), and the unloaded quality factor $Q_0$ was extracted from vector network analyzer $S_{11}$, $S_{22}$, and $S_{21}$ data using a diameter-correction circle-fit for the input and output coupling coefficients. At \SI{2}{\kelvin} the film reached $Q_0 = 1.64\times10^{6}$, corresponding to a surface resistance $R_s = \SI{213}{\micro\ohm}$ for a simulated geometric factor $G=\SI{350}{\ohm}$. This is $\sim$26$\times$ the measured bare-copper baseline of the same body ($Q_0=62{,}000$, $R_s=\SI{5.65}{\milli\ohm}$) and consistent with a simulated bulk-Cu ceiling of $Q_0=77{,}000$ ($R_s=\SI{5.00}{\milli\ohm}$), but roughly 250 times the BCS-limited surface resistance of niobium at this frequency, indicating that surface roughness in the as-sputtered film dominates over the intrinsic BCS term. The sharp transition observed near \SI{9.2}{\kelvin}, the bulk-Nb $T_c$, confirms a stoichiometric film and an effective Ta diffusion barrier against Cu contamination.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Niobium thin films, superconducting cavities, quality factor, surface resistance, BCS theory, axion haloscopes.

\section{Introduction}

Resonant-cavity searches for axion dark matter, and superconducting RF (SRF) accelerator cavities more generally, both benefit from raising the cavity quality factor $Q$ while the cavity sits in a strong static magnetic field or operates at high gradient~\cite{ADMX:2009iij}. Copper is the standard cavity body: its magnetoresistance is weak, it electropolishes into complex shapes, and a bare-copper cavity of identical geometry provides a direct baseline against which any coating is judged~\cite{Braine:2024nzi}. A superconducting film can raise $Q$ by one to two orders of magnitude over copper at zero field, but only if the coating survives the intended operating conditions and adds negligible resistance beyond its intrinsic BCS limit~\cite{Ciovati:2014zti}.

Bulk Nb and NbTi have upper critical fields at or below the fields used in present axion searches, which rules them out of a high-field haloscope on physics grounds alone~\cite{gurevichUseNotUse2011}. Nb$_3$Sn, with $T_c \approx \SI{18.3}{\kelvin}$ and $H_{c2}\approx\SI{30}{\tesla}$, is a leading high-field candidate. The standard route, vapor diffusion of Sn into bulk Nb, gives the highest quality factors reported to date~\cite{posenNb3SnSuperconductingRadiofrequency2017}, but is not compatible with a copper substrate; routes under active development on copper instead include Sn-bronze diffusion~\cite{withanageRapidNbSn2021}, stoichiometric-target magnetron sputtering~\cite{fonnesuRecipeOptimizationSRF2025}, and Nb/Sn co-sputtering~\cite{sayeedFabricationSuperconductingNb3Sn2023}. Before committing to that more complex film chemistry, a sputtered Ta/Nb bilayer on the same copper cavity body serves as a control experiment: because Nb is a well-characterized single-element film, its measured performance isolates how much of the achievable $Q$ is already set by the copper substrate, the cavity seam, and the deposition system, independent of the coating material. This paper reports that control measurement --- the fabrication and RF characterization of a Ta/Nb-coated two-piece copper cavity, benchmarked against the same body's measured and simulated bare-copper performance and against the BCS-limited surface resistance of niobium.

A further motivation is practical. Most published cavity tests in this field use dedicated haloscope-scale facilities with large-bore superconducting solenoids --- \SI{147}{\milli\meter} and \SI{150}{\milli\meter} bores in two recent benchmark results~\cite{posen2023HighQ, alesini2019QUAX} --- which leave ample room for thick-walled, multi-bolt cavity assemblies. The cavity in this work instead had to fit inside the small sample space of a Quantum Design PPMS, constraining its outer dimension to roughly \SI{25}{\milli\meter}, a factor of six smaller. This complements coupon-scale methods used elsewhere in the field --- the SLAC mushroom cavity~\cite{oikawaMushroomCavity2019}, the quadrupole resonator~\cite{luCuBronzeQPR2026}, and the Daresbury choke cavity~\cite{sealChokeCavity2025}, which concentrate the RF field on a flat sample disk rather than a full 3D cavity --- and shows that coatings can instead be screened on an actual cavity geometry in an equally compact, commercially available system. This is useful for axion haloscope prototyping, SRF recipe screening for accelerators, and high-$Q$ RF characterization generally, at the cost of the tighter, harder-to-seal seam discussed in Section~II.

\section{Cavity Design and Fabrication}

The test cavity is a two-piece hexagonal prism machined from bulk OFHC copper, \SI{72}{\milli\meter} tall with a \SI{12.25}{\milli\meter} face length and a minimum wall thickness of \SI{2}{\milli\meter}. The two halves close on four hand-tightened stainless screws and three alignment pegs, operating in the TM$_{010}$ mode near \SI{11}{\giga\hertz}. Each half was cut from bulk copper, mechanically polished, and electropolished before deposition.

The RF surface was coated with a \SI{500}{\nano\meter} Ta diffusion barrier by DC magnetron sputtering at \SI{100}{\watt} with a \SI{-100}{\volt} substrate bias in \SI{10}{\milli\torr} Ar at \SI{200}{\celsius}, followed by a \SI{700}{\nano\meter} Nb layer sputtered at \SI{250}{\watt} in \SI{8}{\milli\torr} Ar, also at \SI{200}{\celsius}. The Nb thickness exceeds three RF penetration depths at \SI{11}{\giga\hertz}, so the measured surface impedance reflects the film rather than the copper beneath it. After deposition, the mating interface between the two halves was hand-polished to close the residual seam gap --- visible as light leakage when the assembled halves were held up to a light source --- which strips the sputtered film from that narrow contact flange. Fig.~\ref{fig:cavity} shows the coated cavity halves; the darker Nb film covers the resonating wall, while the flange appears as bare copper because of this post-deposition polish rather than because it was never coated.

\section{RF Measurement Method}

The assembled cavity was mounted on an RF probe inside a Quantum Design PPMS, enabling temperature control from 2 to \SI{300}{\kelvin} at fields up to \SI{9}{\tesla}; all data reported here were taken at zero applied field. A Keysight PNA vector network analyzer drove the cavity through one antenna (port 1, $S_{11}$) and read out through a second (port 2, $S_{22}$), with transmission recorded as $S_{21}$.

The two-piece geometry allows the cavity to be opened between measurements for surface analysis without rebuilding the RF chain.

Because both ports were non-negligibly coupled, $Q_0$ was extracted with the diameter-correction circle-fit method rather than assuming weak coupling~\cite{KajfezHwan1984}. For each port, the measured $S_{ii}$ trace was fit to a circle in the complex plane with center offset $C$ and radius $R$; the coupling coefficient follows from
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{2R}{|\Gamma_\text{off}|}, \qquad \beta = \frac{d}{2-d}, \qquad |\Gamma_\text{off}| = |C| + R.
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:qcircle}(a)--(b) shows the resulting fits at \SI{2}{\kelvin}, giving $\beta_1 = 0.53$ at the drive port and $\beta_2 = 0.24$ at the pickup port. The loaded quality factor was obtained from a Lorentzian fit to the normalized transmission $|S_{21}|^2$ [Fig.~\ref{fig:qcircle}(c)], using the 3\,dB bandwidth $\Delta f$ about the resonant frequency $f_0$: $Q_L = f_0/\Delta f = 929{,}000$. The unloaded quality factor then follows from
\begin{equation}
Q_0 = Q_L\left(1+\beta_1+\beta_2\right),
\label{eq:q0}
\end{equation}
giving $Q_0 = 1.64\times10^{6}$ at \SI{2}{\kelvin}.

\section{Electromagnetic Simulation}

The cavity geometry was modeled in a finite-element RF solver to confirm the TM$_{010}$ mode identity and to obtain the geometric factor $G$ used to convert measured $Q$ into surface resistance. Fig.~\ref{fig:sim} shows the simulated electric field for the fundamental TM$_{010}$ mode at $f_0=\SI{11.030}{\giga\hertz}$, together with the two nearest higher-order modes, TM$_{011}$ at \SI{11.395}{\giga\hertz} and TM$_{012}$ at \SI{12.399}{\giga\hertz}. Identifying these modes in simulation was necessary to confirm that the resonance tracked during measurement was the intended TM$_{010}$ mode and not a neighboring mode within a few hundred megahertz.

From the TM$_{010}$ eigenmode, the geometric factor was computed from the ratio of the stored magnetic energy to the surface integral of the tangential magnetic field, giving $G=\SI{350}{\ohm}$ for this cavity. Combined with a literature bulk-copper conductivity, the same simulation gives a simulated bare-copper ceiling of $Q_0=77{,}000$ ($R_s = \SI{5.00}{\milli\ohm}$), used in Section~V as a cross-check on the measured copper baseline.

\section{Results and Discussion}

Fig.~\ref{fig:results} shows the measured surface resistance and unloaded quality factor of the Ta/Nb-coated cavity from 2 to \SI{10}{\kelvin} at zero field, obtained from $R_s = G/Q_0$ at each temperature using the circle-fit procedure of Section~III. Three reference curves are overlaid: the BCS-limited surface resistance of niobium at \SI{11}{\giga\hertz}, the measured bare-copper baseline of the same cavity body, and the simulated bulk-copper ceiling from Section~IV.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{rs_q0_vs_T.png}
    \caption{Measured surface resistance (a) and unloaded quality factor (b) of the Ta/Nb-coated cavity versus temperature at 11\,GHz and zero field, compared against BCS Nb, the measured bare-Cu baseline, and the simulated bulk-Cu ceiling.}
    \label{fig:results}
\end{figure*}

At \SI{2}{\kelvin}, the film gives $Q_0=1.64\times10^{6}$ and $R_s=\SI{213}{\micro\ohm}$. Against the measured bare-copper baseline ($Q_0=62{,}000$, $R_s=\SI{5.65}{\milli\ohm}$), this is an improvement of roughly $26\times$ in quality factor, and it is consistent with the independently simulated bulk-Cu ceiling of $Q_0=77{,}000$, confirming that the copper baseline used for comparison is representative of this cavity geometry rather than an outlier. A separate finding on the same bare-copper cavity points to an easy path to a higher baseline still: sealing its seam with indium foil raised $Q_0$ from 55,000 to 62,000, a simple treatment we plan to apply to the coated cavity in future work. Against the BCS-limited value for niobium at this frequency ($R_s \approx \SI{0.58}{\micro\ohm}$ at \SI{2}{\kelvin}, $Q_0\approx 6.0\times10^{8}$), however, the measured film sits roughly 250 times higher in surface resistance. At millikelvin temperatures the BCS term is exponentially suppressed, so a gap of this size at \SI{2}{\kelvin} is too large to attribute to trapped-flux resistance, which for realistic ambient fields remains a comparatively small contribution~\cite{Ciovati:2014zti}; surface roughness in the as-sputtered film, which locally enhances current path length and field at grain boundaries, is the more likely dominant term. Fig.~\ref{fig:roughness} confirms this directly: a Veeco laser-scanning confocal microscope scan of the sputtered Nb surface shows a background roughness of roughly $\pm$\SI{2}{\micro\meter} punctuated by scattered grooves reaching \SIrange{-8}{-11}{\micro\meter} along the line scan marked in red, consistent with polishing or handling scratches that locally enhance the RF surface path length and field. For comparison, electropolished Nb-on-Cu films in the literature reach AFM roughness (Ra) of only \SIrange{6}{21}{\nano\meter}~\cite{Pira2019CuPolish}, two to three orders of magnitude below what is measured here.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{roughness.png}
    \caption{Laser-scanning confocal microscope (Veeco) height map of the sputtered Nb surface (top; red line marks the scan location) and the corresponding line-scan height profile (bottom), showing $\pm$\SI{2}{\micro\meter} background roughness with scattered grooves reaching \SIrange{-8}{-11}{\micro\meter}.}
    \label{fig:roughness}
\end{figure}

Approaching the transition, $R_s$ rises gradually before increasing sharply near \SI{9.2}{\kelvin}, matching the bulk-Nb critical temperature. Above $T_c$, $Q_0$ falls to order $10^{4}$, below the bare-copper baseline, because normal-state Nb is more resistive than annealed copper and the \SI{700}{\nano\meter} film exceeds its normal-state skin depth at \SI{11}{\giga\hertz}, so the RF fields see only the lossy normal-state Nb and not the copper substrate beneath it. That the transition sits at the bulk-Nb $T_c$, rather than at a suppressed value indicative of Cu interdiffusion, confirms that the \SI{500}{\nano\meter} Ta layer is an effective diffusion barrier and that the sputtered film is close to stoichiometric bulk Nb.

These results establish the two-piece hexagonal cavity, combined with the PPMS-based circle-fit measurement of Section~III, as a fast-turnaround platform for screening superconducting coatings: it isolates the substrate, seam, and deposition-system contributions to $Q$ from the intrinsic quality of the film before a more complex, higher-$H_{c2}$ coating such as Nb$_3$Sn is attempted on the same body.

\section{Conclusion}

A two-piece hexagonal OFHC copper cavity coated with a sputtered Ta/Nb bilayer was characterized at \SI{11}{\giga\hertz} from 2 to \SI{10}{\kelvin}. Using a diameter-correction circle-fit to extract coupling coefficients from $S_{11}$ and $S_{22}$, the unloaded quality factor at \SI{2}{\kelvin} was found to be $Q_0=1.64\times10^{6}$ ($R_s=\SI{213}{\micro\ohm}$), roughly $26\times$ the cavity's own measured bare-copper baseline and consistent with an independently simulated bulk-Cu ceiling, but approximately 250 times the BCS-limited surface resistance of niobium at this frequency, an excess more consistent with as-sputtered surface roughness than with trapped-flux resistance. The sharp transition observed at the bulk-Nb $T_c$ of \SI{9.2}{\kelvin} confirms a stoichiometric film and an effective Ta diffusion barrier. Demonstrating this within the \SI{25}{\milli\meter} sample space of a commercial PPMS, rather than a purpose-built haloscope magnet with six times the bore, shows that coatings can be screened without dedicated large-bore infrastructure. This measurement isolates the residual, non-BCS loss of the deposition process on this cavity body, and establishes a baseline against which future work on the same platform --- sealing the coated cavity's seam with indium as already done for the bare-copper baseline, direct roughness characterization of the sputtered film, HiPIMS-deposited Nb, and Nb$_3$Sn by the Cu-Sn route --- will be evaluated.
