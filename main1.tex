\begin{abstract}

Superconducting thin-film coatings can raise the quality factor of copper haloscope and accelerator cavities by orders of magnitude. Because many candidate materials and deposition processes exist, fast and reliable ways to measure the RF performance of a film are needed. Here we report the fabrication and \SI{11}{\giga\hertz} RF characterization of a compact two-piece hexagonal oxygen-free high-conductivity (OFHC) copper cavity sputter-coated with a \SI{500}{\nano\meter} Ta diffusion barrier and a \SI{700}{\nano\meter} Nb film. The cavity fits inside a commercial Quantum Design Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS) and was measured from 2 to \SI{10}{\kelvin}; the unloaded quality factor $Q_0$ was extracted from vector network analyzer $S_{11}$, $S_{22}$, and $S_{21}$ data using the antenna coupling coefficients. The bare Cu cavity reached $Q_0=55{,}000$ ($R_s=\SI{6.67}{\milli\ohm}$), constant from 2 to \SI{10}{\kelvin} and within $\sim$20\% of the anomalous-skin-effect limit for Cu at this frequency. The Ta/Nb-coated cavity reached $Q_0 = 1.64\times10^{6}$ at \SI{2}{\kelvin}, a $\sim$30-fold improvement, corresponding to $R_s = \SI{224}{\micro\ohm}$ (using the simulated geometric factor $G=\SI{367}{\ohm}$). This is roughly two orders of magnitude below the $Q_0 \sim 10^{8}$ expected for a bulk Nb cavity at this frequency without magnetic shielding. Opening the cavity after the RF test revealed poor Nb coverage at the inner hexagon edges and micrometer-scale substrate roughness as the likely $Q$-limiting defects, showing how the two-piece design links an RF result directly to film defects.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Developing next-generation superconducting radio-frequency (SRF) cavities beyond high-purity Nb for higher-temperature $\sim$4 K operations increasingly depends on engineered surfaces and thin-film superconductors that can exceed the performance and cost limits of conventional bulk niobium~\cite{valenteFelicianoReview2016}. Candidate technologies include Nb$_3$Sn on Cu, Nb/Cu, multilayer superconducting structures, and other high-$T_c$ or high-superheating-field materials~\cite{valenteFelicianoReview2016, posen2023HighQ}. Because many of these materials cannot be fabricated as bulk cavities, thin-film deposition onto Nb or Cu substrates offers an attractive pathway to higher operating temperatures, reduces cryogenic demand, and potentially improves RF performance.

A fundamental bottleneck, however, is how quickly researchers can evaluate candidate materials and deposition processes under realistic RF conditions. Thin-film development for SRF cavities involves a large, multidimensional processing space, including deposition technique, substrate condition, deposition temperature, film thickness, composition, microstructure, interface quality, heat treatment, surface processing, and, importantly, deposition on 3D cavity shapes. Conventional material characterization techniques such as XRD, SEM, transport measurements, magnetometry, and DC susceptibility provide essential superconducting material information but cannot directly determine the RF surface resistance $R_{s}$, field-dependent losses, or RF-induced degradation that ultimately governs SRF cavity performance.  Consequently, RF testing remains an indispensable bridge between materials development and cavity implementation.

No direct method exists to evaluate structure-processing-performance relationships for SRF surfaces and thin films. Significant efforts in thermometry and destructive post-test coupon cutting from problem areas are state-of-the-art techniques to bridge defects to SRF performance~\cite{knobloch1995thermometry, trenikhinaSRF2013, ciovatiAnlageGurevich2013, posenTrenikhina2017NbSnPerformance, bianchiVenturiniDelsolaro2023, geCooley2011Replica}. Sample-based RF systems, particularly cavity-based systems, are needed to address this problem by exposing relatively small material samples to RF magnetic fields ($B_{RF}$), enabling measurements of $R_s(T, B_{RF}, f)$. Current SRF thin-film material characterization techniques rely on concentrating the RF field onto a flat 2D disc sample: quadrupole resonator (QPR)~\cite{del2018redesign, monroy2019design,ge2026design,zhao2026multiphysics}, mushroom cavities~\cite{nantista2005test,xie2013development,maniscalco2016new}, and choke cavity~\cite{seal2024high}. An alternative approach is to design 3D shapes with split cells, such as the SWELL cavity at CERN~\cite{peaugerSWELL2022} and the 6\,GHz Daresbury split cell~\cite{leicesterSplit6GHz2023}. The cavity reported here follows this general split-cell 3D approach, using a two-piece hexagonal prism geometry.

Nb and NbTi have upper critical fields at or below the fields used in present axion searches, which rules them out of a high-field haloscope on physics grounds alone~\cite{gurevichUseNotUse2011}. Nb$_3$Sn, with $T_c \approx \SI{18.3}{\kelvin}$ and $H_{c2}\approx\SI{30}{\tesla}$, is a leading high-field candidate. The standard route, vapor diffusion of Sn into bulk Nb, gives the highest quality factors reported to date~\cite{posenNb3SnSuperconductingRadiofrequency2017}, but is not compatible with a copper substrate; routes under active development on copper instead include Sn-bronze diffusion~\cite{withanageRapidNbSn2021}, stoichiometric-target magnetron sputtering~\cite{fonnesuRecipeOptimizationSRF2025}, and Nb/Sn co-sputtering~\cite{sayeedFabricationSuperconductingNb3Sn2023}. Nb$_3$Sn films are left for future work, as they had poor quality factors ($< 10^5$) at zero magnetic field, so simpler Nb films were measured here as a control experiment. The Nb film and bare Cu were measured in the same Cu cavity body and compared with simulated bulk Cu and with the expected performance of bulk Nb under similar conditions (no magnetic shielding, film exposed to air before testing).

The cavity in this work fit inside the small sample space of a Quantum Design PPMS, constraining its outer dimension to \SI{26}{\milli\meter}, a factor of six smaller compared to other benchmark results~\cite{posen2023HighQ, alesini2019QUAX}. The cylindrical PPMS cavity design was first proposed by Thomas Braine~\cite{Braine:2024nzi}, and iterated into a hexagonal prism design used for this work~\cite{juliaoNb3SnThesis2026}. This PPMS-compatible design allows for rapid screening of the quality factor vs temperature and magnetic field for coatings using a compact, commercially available system.


\section{Methods}
\subsection{Cavity Fabrication and Initial Preparation}
\label{sec:fabrication}


The two-piece hexagonal prism cavity is machined from bulk oxygen-free high-conductivity copper, is \SI{72}{\milli\meter} tall with a \SI{12.25}{\milli\meter} inner wall face length and a minimum wall thickness of \SI{2}{\milli\meter}. The two halves close with four hand-tightened stainless screws and three alignment pegs, operating in the TM$_{010}$ mode near \SI{11}{\giga\hertz}. Each half was mechanically polished by hand with 400 to 1200 grit and electropolished in Phosphoric:butanol (3:2) at 45 C with a stir bar at 600 RPM before deposition.

\subsection{Superconducting Thin Film Deposition}
\label{sec:deposition}

The RF surface was coated with a \SI{500}{\nano\meter} Ta diffusion barrier by DC magnetron sputtering at \SI{100}{\watt} with a \SI{-100}{\volt} substrate bias in \SI{10}{\milli\torr} Ar at \SI{200}{\celsius}, followed by a \SI{700}{\nano\meter} Nb layer sputtered at \SI{250}{\watt} in \SI{8}{\milli\torr} Ar, also at \SI{200}{\celsius}. After deposition, we hand-polished the mating interface between the two halves to close the seam gap; however, light was still visible when we held the assembled halves up to a light source. Fig.~\ref{fig:cavity} shows the coated cavity halves; the darker Nb film covers the resonating wall, while the interface appears as bare copper due to the post-deposition polish.

\subsection{RF Measurement Setup}
\label{sec:measurement}


The assembled cavity was mounted on an RF probe inside a Quantum Design PPMS~\cite{Braine:2024thesis}, enabling temperature control from 2 to \SI{300}{\kelvin} at fields up to \SI{9}{\tesla}; all data reported here were taken at zero applied field. A Keysight PNA vector network analyzer drove the cavity through one antenna (port 1, $S_{11}$) and read out through a second (port 2, $S_{22}$), with transmission recorded as $S_{21}$. The two-piece geometry allows opening the cavity before and after measurement and deposition. 

Because both ports were non-negligibly coupled, $Q_0$ was extracted with the diameter-correction circle-fit method rather than assuming weak coupling~\cite{KajfezHwan1984}. For each port, the measured $S_{ii}$ trace was fit to a circle in the complex plane with center offset $C$ and radius $R$; the coupling coefficient follows from
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{2R}{|\Gamma_\text{off}|}, \qquad \beta = \frac{d}{2-d}, \qquad |\Gamma_\text{off}| = |C| + R.
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}

\section{Results}
\subsection{Electromagnetic Simulation of Cavities}
\label{sec:simulation}

We modeled the cavity geometry in COMSOL to confirm the TM$_{010}$ mode identity and obtain the geometric factor $G$ used to convert measured $Q$ into surface resistance. Fig.~\ref{fig:modes} shows the simulated field for the fundamental TM$_{010}$ mode at $f_0=\SI{11.01}{\giga\hertz}$, together with the two nearest higher-order modes, TM$_{011}$ at \SI{11.37}{\giga\hertz} and TM$_{012}$ at \SI{12.34}{\giga\hertz}. Identifying these modes in simulation was necessary to confirm which resonance mode we were tracking.

Combined with a literature bulk-copper conductivity, the same simulation gives a simulated bare-copper ceiling of $Q_0=73{,}000$ (input $R_s = \SI{5.00}{\milli\ohm}$), used in Section~\ref{sec:Qfactor} as a cross-check on the measured copper baseline.

\subsection{Quality Factor Measurements}
\label{sec:Qfactor}

The coupling coefficients of the Ta/Nb-coated cavity at \SI{2}{\kelvin} were $\beta_1 = 0.53$ at the drive port and $\beta_2 = 0.24$ at the pickup port. The loaded quality factor was obtained from a Lorentzian fit to the normalized transmission $|S_{21}|^2$, using the 3\,dB bandwidth $\Delta f$ about the resonant frequency $f_0$: $Q_L = f_0/\Delta f = 929{,}000$. The unloaded quality factor then follows from
\begin{equation}
Q_0 = Q_L\left(1+\beta_1+\beta_2\right),
\label{eq:q0}
\end{equation}
giving $Q_0 = 1.64\times10^{6}$ at \SI{2}{\kelvin}. The same procedure was applied to the bare-Cu cavity; the results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:Q}.

The measured bare-Cu baseline ($Q_0=55{,}000$, $R_s=\SI{6.67}{\milli\ohm}$) is 25\% below the simulated $Q_0=73{,}000$, but close to the physical limit for cold copper. At \SI{11}{\giga\hertz} and cryogenic temperature the electron mean free path in OFHC Cu ($\sim$\SI{4}{\micro\meter} for RRR\,$\approx$\,100) is far longer than the skin depth, so the surface resistance saturates in the anomalous-skin-effect regime at $R_s \approx \SI{5.6}{\milli\ohm}$ for diffuse surface scattering ($Q_0\approx66{,}000$), independent of purity~\cite{reuterSondheimer1948, chambers1952, Braine:2024thesis}. The measured $R_s$ lies within $\sim$20\% of this floor, consistent with $Q_0$ being constant from 2 to \SI{10}{\kelvin}; the simulation input of \SI{5.00}{\milli\ohm} sits slightly below it. On a separate, similarly electropolished cylindrical cavity, sealing the seam with indium foil raised the bare-Cu $Q_0$ from 55,000 to 62,000, showing that seam leakage is measurable even for copper.

The Ta/Nb-coated cavity reached $Q_0=1.64\times10^{6}$ ($R_s=\SI{224}{\micro\ohm}$) at \SI{2}{\kelvin}, about 30 times the bare-Cu value. For comparison, a bulk Nb cavity at \SI{11}{\giga\hertz} and \SI{2}{\kelvin} without magnetic shielding would be expected to reach $R_s \approx$ 1--4\,\si{\micro\ohm} ($Q_0 \sim 10^{8}$). In this estimate, the BCS surface resistance, scaled approximately as $f^2$ from measurements at \SI{1.3}{\giga\hertz}~\cite{padamseeRFSuperconductivity2008}, and the resistance from fully trapped ambient flux ($\sim$0.5\,G) contribute comparably, since at this frequency the trapped-flux sensitivity of Nb approaches its frequency-independent flux-flow value of a few n$\Omega$/mG~\cite{checchinFrequencyDependence2018}. The film is therefore roughly two orders of magnitude worse than an equivalent unshielded bulk Nb cavity.

$Q_0$ drops sharply near \SI{8.7}{\kelvin}, about \SI{0.5}{\kelvin} below the bulk-Nb $T_c$, and falls to order $10^{4}$ above it. Post-test laser-scanning confocal microscopy of the opened cavity showed thin or missing Nb along the inner edges where adjacent hexagon faces meet (Fig.~\ref{fig:cavity}b). A \SI{1.2}{\milli\meter} line scan of the back wall gave $R_a = \SI{0.91}{\micro\meter}$, $R_q = \SI{1.43}{\micro\meter}$, and $R_z = \SI{9.98}{\micro\meter}$, more than ten times the film thickness.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Origin of the Low $Q_0$}

Several mechanisms can account for the gap between the measured film and bulk Nb. The most direct is exposed copper. Because bare Cu has $R_s$ about 30 times higher than the measured film, uncoated Cu covering only $\sim$3\% of the $|H|^2$-weighted wall area would by itself produce the measured \SI{224}{\micro\ohm}. The wall current is reduced near the hexagon edges, so the physical area involved can be larger, but the poorly coated edges found in the post-test inspection remain a plausible major loss. Substrate roughness compounds this: grooves up to \SI{10}{\micro\meter} deep cannot be conformally covered by a \SI{700}{\nano\meter} film, whereas electropolished Cu substrates for Nb films reach $R_a$ of \SIrange{6}{21}{\nano\meter}~\cite{Pira2019CuPolish}. The suppressed $T_c$ shows that the film itself can also be improved, for example with a thicker layer or HiPIMS~\cite{leithHiPIMSNbCu2023}. Finally, RF leakage through the seam, where light was still visible after assembly, and flux trapped from ambient or remanent PPMS magnet fields add losses that a superconducting film exposes far more than copper.

\subsection{Coating Coverage at the Inner Edges}

The inspection showed no sign of film debonding; instead, the Nb is thin or absent where the underlying Cu surface is worst, at the inner edges where adjacent hexagon faces meet. These sharp internal corners are the hardest regions to polish by hand and are recessed during electropolishing, so they retain the deepest machining marks, which line-of-sight sputtering then shadows. Sharp internal edges are thus a limitation of the hexagonal geometry for substrate preparation, which can be addressed by machining a fillet radius into the inner edges, improving the as-machined finish, and depositing at multiple angles. The mating interface between the halves, by contrast, was polished only to reduce the seam gap.

\subsection{Limitations of the Design}

The halves are assembled in air rather than in an inert environment, so the fresh film is exposed to oxygen and moisture before testing. This rules out straightforward testing of air- or moisture-sensitive superconductors unless assembly can be moved into a glovebox and the cavity sealed before transfer to the PPMS. The main limitation, however, is sealing the seam. In the TM$_{010}$ mode the wall current flows parallel to the seam, so an ideal seam does not interrupt it, but a finite gap still lets field leak into the joint. Indium foil improved the bare-Cu cavity, yet indium is a poor general solution: it becomes normal above \SI{3.4}{\kelvin} and in modest magnetic fields, adding its own loss in exactly the field-dependent measurements this platform is meant for, and it must be removed between iterations without damaging the film. Any external clamp or casing used to improve the seal also competes for space in the \SI{26}{\milli\meter} PPMS sample chamber.

\subsection{A Test Bed for Film Development}

Despite these limitations, the cavity works as a test bed. Any deposition process yields an RF figure of merit, and because the RF-tested surface can be inspected directly afterwards, rather than through thermometry maps or destructive coupon cutting, losses can be traced to specific defects. For a new film, the first question is simply whether a reasonable $R_s$ can be obtained, and this approach answers it quickly in a widely available instrument. Recoating, reassembling, and retesting the same cavity is a natural next step.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have developed a compact \SI{11}{\giga\hertz} two-piece cavity that enables quality-factor evaluation of superconducting films deposited on curved Cu surfaces in a widely available PPMS with a \SI{26}{\milli\meter} sample chamber. The bare Cu cavity reached $Q_0=55{,}000$, within $\sim$20\% of the anomalous-skin-effect limit for copper. Our initial Ta/Nb film on Cu reached $Q_0=1.64\times10^{6}$ at \SI{2}{\kelvin}, 30 times the bare-Cu value but roughly two orders of magnitude below an unshielded bulk Nb cavity. The compact design enables post-mortem evaluation of the RF-tested film with standard optical, electron, and X-ray techniques, which here revealed two major $Q$-limiting defects: substrate roughness ($R_a=\SI{0.91}{\micro\meter}$, grooves up to \SI{10}{\micro\meter}) and poor Nb coverage at the inner hexagon edges. The main limitations are air exposure during assembly and sealing the seam without adding loss.
