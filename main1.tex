\begin{abstract}

Superconducting thin-film coatings on copper accelerator structures offer a direct route to compact, high-performance, low-cost superconducting radio-frequency (SRF) cavities. This makes the technology attractive for compact commercial accelerators that require 1–10 MeV beams. Final device performance depends on many coupled variables, including the choice of superconductor, the deposition method and substrate preparation. Exploring this multi-variable space efficiently requires techniques that evaluate an SRF surface quickly and against quantitative metrics. Here we measure the temperature-dependent quality factor, $Q_0(T)$, of a compact hexagonal copper cavity before and after deposition of a Nb thin film, using the 26 mm bore of a standard Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS). The cavity's split geometry allows the film surface to be characterised after RF testing. This directly links process variables to film structure, properties and RF performance. The approach applies to several classes of superconductors. Together, the cavity design, post-measurement surface analysis and fast RF testing in a standard laboratory instrument enable rapid development iteration.

\end{abstract}

Niobium thin films, superconducting cavities, quality factor, surface resistance, split-cell cavity, axion haloscopes.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Developing next-generation superconducting radio-frequency (SRF) cavities beyond high-purity Nb for higher-temperature $\sim$4 K operations increasingly depends on engineered surfaces and thin-film superconductors that can exceed the performance and cost limits of conventional bulk niobium~\cite{valenteFelicianoReview2016}. Candidate technologies include Nb$_3$Sn on Cu, Nb/Cu, multilayer superconducting structures, and other high-$T_c$ or high-superheating-field materials~\cite{valenteFelicianoReview2016, posen2023HighQ}. Because many of these materials cannot be fabricated as bulk cavities, thin-film deposition onto Nb or Cu substrates offers an attractive pathway to higher operating temperatures, reduces cryogenic demand, and potentially improves RF performance.

A fundamental bottleneck, however, is how quickly researchers can evaluate candidate materials and deposition processes under realistic RF conditions. Thin-film development for SRF cavities involves a large, multidimensional processing space, including deposition technique, substrate condition, deposition temperature, film thickness, composition, microstructure, interface quality, heat treatment, surface processing, and, importantly, deposition on 3D cavity shapes. Conventional material characterization techniques such as XRD, SEM, transport measurements, magnetometry, and DC susceptibility provide essential superconducting material information but cannot directly determine the RF surface resistance $R_{s}$, field-dependent losses, or RF-induced degradation that ultimately governs SRF cavity performance.  Consequently, RF testing remains an indispensable bridge between materials development and cavity implementation.

No direct method exists to evaluate structure-processing-performance relationships for SRF surfaces and thin films. Significant efforts in thermometry and destructive post-test coupon cutting from problem areas are state-of-the-art techniques to bridge defects to SRF performance~\cite{knobloch1995thermometry, trenikhinaSRF2013, ciovatiAnlageGurevich2013, posenTrenikhina2017NbSnPerformance, bianchiVenturiniDelsolaro2023, geCooley2011Replica}. Sample-based RF systems, particularly cavity-based systems, are needed to address this problem by exposing relatively small material samples to RF magnetic fields ($B_{RF}$), enabling measurements of $R_s(T, B_{RF}, f)$. Current SRF thin-film material characterization techniques rely on concentrating the RF field onto a flat 2D disc sample: quadrupole resonator (QPR)~\cite{del2018redesign, monroy2019design,ge2026design,zhao2026multiphysics}, mushroom cavities~\cite{nantista2005test,xie2013development,maniscalco2016new}, and choke cavity~\cite{seal2024high}. An alternative approach is to design 3D shapes with split cells, such as the SWELL cavity at CERN~\cite{peaugerSWELL2022} and the 6\,GHz Daresbury split cell~\cite{leicesterSplit6GHz2023}. The cavity reported here follows this general split-cell 3D approach, using a two-piece hexagonal prism geometry.

Nb and NbTi have upper critical fields at or below the fields used in present axion searches, which rules them out of a high-field haloscope on physics grounds alone~\cite{gurevichUseNotUse2011}. Nb$_3$Sn, with $T_c \approx \SI{18.3}{\kelvin}$ and $H_{c2}\approx\SI{30}{\tesla}$, is a leading high-field candidate. The standard route, vapor diffusion of Sn into bulk Nb, gives the highest quality factors reported to date~\cite{posenNb3SnSuperconductingRadiofrequency2017}, but is not compatible with a copper substrate; routes under active development on copper instead include Sn-bronze diffusion~\cite{withanageRapidNbSn2021}, stoichiometric-target magnetron sputtering~\cite{fonnesuRecipeOptimizationSRF2025}, and Nb/Sn co-sputtering~\cite{sayeedFabricationSuperconductingNb3Sn2023}. Nb$_3$Sn films are left for future work, as they had poor quality factors ($< 10^5$) at zero magnetic field, so simpler Nb films were measured here as a control experiment. The Nb film and bare Cu were measured in the same Cu cavity body and compared with simulated bulk Cu and with the expected performance of bulk Nb under similar conditions (no magnetic shielding, film exposed to air before testing).

The cavity in this work fit inside the small sample space of a Quantum Design PPMS, constraining its outer dimension to \SI{26}{\milli\meter}, a factor of six smaller compared to other benchmark results~\cite{posen2023HighQ, alesini2019QUAX}. The cylindrical PPMS cavity design was first proposed by Thomas Braine~\cite{Braine:2024nzi}, and iterated into a hexagonal prism design used for this work~\cite{juliaoNb3SnThesis2026}. This PPMS-compatible design allows for rapid screening of the quality factor vs temperature and magnetic field for coatings using a compact, commercially available system.


\section{Methods}
\subsection{Cavity Fabrication and Initial Preparation}
\label{sec:fabrication}


The two-piece hexagonal prism cavity is machined from bulk oxygen-free high-conductivity copper, is \SI{72}{\milli\meter} tall (\SI{52}{\milli\meter} inner length) with a \SI{11.6}{\milli\meter} inner wall face width and a minimum wall thickness of \SI{2}{\milli\meter}. The two halves close with four hand-tightened stainless screws and three alignment pegs, and the cavity operates in the TM$_{010}$ mode near \SI{11}{\giga\hertz}. Before deposition, each half was mechanically polished by hand with 400 to 1200 grit and electropolished in phosphoric acid:butanol (3:2) at \SI{45}{\celsius} with a stir bar at 600\,RPM.

\subsection{Superconducting Thin Film Deposition}
\label{sec:deposition}

The RF surface was coated with a \SI{500}{\nano\meter} Ta diffusion barrier by DC magnetron sputtering at \SI{100}{\watt} with a \SI{-100}{\volt} substrate bias in \SI{10}{\milli\torr} Ar at \SI{200}{\celsius}, followed by a \SI{700}{\nano\meter} Nb layer sputtered at \SI{250}{\watt} in \SI{8}{\milli\torr} Ar, also at \SI{200}{\celsius}. After deposition, we hand-polished the mating interface between the two halves to close the seam gap; however, light was still visible when we held the assembled halves up to a light source. Fig.~\ref{fig:cavity} shows the coated cavity halves; the darker Nb film covers the resonating wall, while the interface appears as bare copper due to the post-deposition polish.
\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{cavity_photo.png}
    \caption{a) The two-piece hexagonal Cu/Ta/Nb cavity after sputtering the Ta diffusion barrier and Nb film. b) Uncoated Cu at an inner edge where adjacent hexagon faces meet.}
    \label{fig:cavity}
\end{figure}

\subsection{RF Measurement Setup}
\label{sec:measurement}


The assembled cavity was mounted on an RF probe inside a Quantum Design PPMS~\cite{Braine:2024thesis}, enabling temperature control from 2 to \SI{300}{\kelvin} at fields up to \SI{9}{\tesla}; all data reported here were taken at zero applied field, with a remanent field below 1\,Oe. A Keysight PNA vector network analyzer drove the cavity through one antenna (port 1, $S_{11}$) and read out through a second (port 2, $S_{22}$), with transmission recorded as $S_{21}$. The two-piece geometry allows opening the cavity before and after measurement and deposition. 

Because both ports were non-negligibly coupled, we extracted $Q_0$ using the diameter-correction circle-fit method rather than assuming weak coupling~\cite{KajfezHwan1984}. For each port, the measured $S_{ii}$ trace was fit to a circle in the complex plane with center offset $C$ and radius $R$; the coupling coefficient follows from
\begin{equation}
d = \frac{2R}{|\Gamma_\text{off}|}, \qquad \beta = \frac{d}{2-d}, \qquad |\Gamma_\text{off}| = |C| + R.
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}

\section{Results}
\subsection{Electromagnetic Simulation of Cavities}
\label{sec:simulation}

We modeled the cavity geometry in COMSOL to confirm the TM$_{010}$ mode identity and obtain the geometric factor $G$ for converting measured $Q$ to surface resistance. Fig.~\ref{fig:modes} shows the simulated field for the fundamental TM$_{010}$ mode at $f_0=\SI{11.01}{\giga\hertz}$, together with the two nearest higher-order modes, TM$_{011}$ at \SI{11.37}{\giga\hertz} and TM$_{012}$ at \SI{12.34}{\giga\hertz}, which identify the measured resonance.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{sim_modes.png}
    \caption{Simulated normalized $|E|$ of the three lowest-order modes, left to right: TM$_{010}$, TM$_{011}$, and TM$_{012}$.}
    \label{fig:modes}
\end{figure}

From the TM$_{010}$ eigenmode, we computed the geometric factor from the ratio of stored magnetic energy to the surface integral of the tangential magnetic field, giving $G=\SI{367}{\ohm}$ for this cavity. Combined with a literature bulk-copper conductivity, the same simulation gives a simulated bare-copper ceiling of $Q_0=73{,}000$ (input $R_s = \SI{5.00}{\milli\ohm}$), used in Section~\ref{sec:Qfactor} as a cross-check on the measured copper baseline.

\subsection{Quality Factor Measurements}
\label{sec:Qfactor}

The coupling coefficients of the Ta/Nb-coated cavity at \SI{2}{\kelvin} were $\beta_1 = 0.53$ at the drive port and $\beta_2 = 0.24$ at the pickup port. We obtained the loaded quality factor from a Lorentzian fit to the normalized transmission $|S_{21}|^2$, using the 3\,dB bandwidth $\Delta f$ about the resonant frequency $f_0$: $Q_L = f_0/\Delta f = 929{,}000$. The unloaded quality factor then follows from
\begin{equation}
Q_0 = Q_L\left(1+\beta_1+\beta_2\right),
\label{eq:q0}
\end{equation}
giving $Q_0 = 1.64\times10^{6}$ at \SI{2}{\kelvin}. We applied the same procedure to the bare-Cu cavity; the results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:Q}.

The measured bare-Cu baseline ($Q_0=55{,}000$, $R_s=\SI{6.67}{\milli\ohm}$) is 25\% below the simulated $Q_0=73{,}000$, but close to the physical limit for cold copper: at \SI{11}{\giga\hertz} the electron mean free path in cold OFHC Cu far exceeds the skin depth, so $R_s$ saturates in the anomalous-skin-effect regime at $\approx\SI{5.6}{\milli\ohm}$ ($Q_0\approx66{,}000$), independent of purity~\cite{reuterSondheimer1948, chambers1952, Braine:2024thesis}. The measured $R_s$ is within $\sim$20\% of this floor, consistent with $Q_0$ being constant from 2 to \SI{10}{\kelvin}. On a separate cylindrical cavity, sealing the seam with indium foil raised the bare-Cu $Q_0$ from 55,000 to 62,000.

The Ta/Nb-coated cavity reached $Q_0=1.64\times10^{6}$ ($R_s=\SI{224}{\micro\ohm}$) at \SI{2}{\kelvin}, about 30 times the bare-Cu value. The BCS limit for Nb at \SI{11}{\giga\hertz} and \SI{2}{\kelvin} is $R_s\approx\SI{0.58}{\micro\ohm}$ ($Q_0\approx6.3\times10^{8}$, Fig.~\ref{fig:results})~\cite{padamseeRFSuperconductivity2008}; a real unshielded bulk Nb cavity adds comparable loss from trapped ambient flux ($\sim$0.5\,G at a few n$\Omega$/mG~\cite{checchinFrequencyDependence2018}), for a total $R_s \approx$ 1--4\,\si{\micro\ohm} ($Q_0 \sim 10^{8}$). The film is roughly two orders of magnitude worse than such a cavity.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{images/Quality.png}
    \caption{Measured unloaded quality factor of the Ta/Nb-coated cavity versus temperature at \SI{11}{\giga\hertz} and zero field, compared against BCS Nb, the measured bare-Cu baseline, and the simulated bulk-Cu ceiling.}
    \label{fig:results}
\end{figure}

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:results}, $Q_0$ drops sharply near \SI{8.7}{\kelvin}, about \SI{0.5}{\kelvin} below the bulk-Nb $T_c$, and falls to order $10^{4}$ above it. Post-test laser-scanning confocal microscopy of the opened cavity showed thin or missing Nb along the inner edges where adjacent hexagon faces meet (Fig.~\ref{fig:cavity}b). A \SI{1.2}{\milli\meter} line scan of the back wall gave $R_a = \SI{0.91}{\micro\meter}$, $R_q = \SI{1.43}{\micro\meter}$, and $R_z = \SI{9.98}{\micro\meter}$, more than ten times the film thickness.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Origin of the Low $Q_0$}

The most direct explanation for the gap between the film and bulk Nb is exposed copper. The measured \SI{224}{\micro\ohm} is only $\sim$3\% of the \SI{6.67}{\milli\ohm} of bare Cu, so if bare Cu replaced Nb on just $\sim$3\% of the wall, weighted by local RF current, it alone would account for the measured loss. The current is weaker near the hexagon edges, so a larger fraction of the wall would have to be bare there, but the poorly coated edges remain a plausible major loss. Substrate roughness compounds this: grooves up to \SI{10}{\micro\meter} deep cannot be conformally covered by a \SI{700}{\nano\meter} film, whereas electropolished Cu substrates for Nb films reach $R_a$ of \SIrange{6}{21}{\nano\meter}~\cite{Pira2019CuPolish}. The suppressed $T_c$ shows the film itself can also be improved, e.g., by HiPIMS~\cite{leithHiPIMSNbCu2023}. Finally, RF leakage through the seam, where light was still visible after assembly, adds loss that a superconducting film exposes far more than copper. Trapped flux is not the main loss: below 1\,Oe remanent field it adds at most a few \si{\micro\ohm}.

\subsection{Coating Coverage at the Inner Edges}

The inspection showed no sign of film debonding; instead, the Nb is thin or absent where the underlying Cu surface is worst, at the inner edges where adjacent hexagon faces meet. These sharp internal corners are the hardest regions to polish by hand and are recessed during electropolishing, so they retain the deepest machining marks, which line-of-sight sputtering then shadows. Sharp internal edges are thus a limitation of the hexagonal geometry, which a fillet radius on the inner edges, a better as-machined finish, and multi-angle deposition can address.

\subsection{Current Limitations that need to be addressed}

Currently, the  halves are assembled in air, so air- or moisture-sensitive superconductors cannot easily be tested unless assembly moves into a glovebox and the cavity is sealed before transfer to the PPMS. Sealing of the two halves is a challenge. The design simulations show that the  TM$_{010}$ wall current flows parallel to the seam, but a finite gap leads to field leak into joints. Indium foil helped the bare-Cu cavity, but it turns normal above \SI{3.4}{\kelvin} and in modest fields. An external clamp or casing could improve the seal but competes for space in the \SI{26}{\milli\meter} PPMS sample chamber.



\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have developed a compact \SI{11}{\giga\hertz} two-piece cavity that enables quality-factor, and $R_s (T)$ evaluation of superconducting films deposited on curved Cu surfaces in a widely available PPMS with a \SI{26}{\milli\meter} sample chamber. We deposited a Nb film on a Cu cavity and evaluated the deposited film after the RF measurement. 
This technique enables a faster process and surface and substrate development cycle for superconducting films. 
The two- piece compact split- cell cavity enables correlative post- measurement material characterization which enables structure performance property relationships of a SRF surface layer.
